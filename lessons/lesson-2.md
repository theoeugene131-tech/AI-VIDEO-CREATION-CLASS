# Lesson 2: From Idea to a Script AI Can Use

**Goal:** Write a 30-second, one-line-per-shot script for a real product or service, with prompts that produce usable clips.
**Video length:** ~15 minutes

---

## Audio narration script

In lesson one, you generated clips with whatever came to mind. Today you learn the difference between a prompt and a plan. This is the single most important lesson in the course, because it is the difference between clips that go nowhere and videos that get watched — and paid for.

Section 1. Why most AI videos look random. AI video models do one thing well: a short clip of one subject doing one action. When your prompt asks for three things at once, the model tries to blend them, and you get mush — a person with too many fingers, a room that changes shape, a story that goes nowhere. [show bad example clip]

Section 2. Think like a director, not a novelist. A novelist writes flowing paragraphs. A director thinks in shots. One line per shot. Each line describes one moment: who or what is in the frame, what they are doing, how the camera moves, and what the light looks like. If your script reads like a story, rewrite it until it reads like a shot list.

Section 3. The anatomy of a usable prompt. There are four parts. Subject: what or who is in the shot — a plate of jollof rice. Action: what it is doing — being served, steam rising. Camera: how the frame moves — a slow push-in, static, or a tracking shot from the side. Style: the look — warm cinematic light, phone-cam feel, 4K. Now compare two prompts. Bad: "Make a cool video of a restaurant." Good: "Close-up of jollof rice being served, steam rising, slow push-in, warm cinematic light." Same idea, completely different result. The rule: if you cannot say it in one sentence, it is two shots.

Section 4. Write a 30-second script. A 30-second video is only five to six shots, each three to six seconds long. Let us write one for a real business — a small restaurant in Lagos. [write it live on screen] Shot one: "Exterior of the restaurant in morning light, slow push-in toward the sign, cinematic." Shot two: "Close-up of jollof rice being served, steam rising, warm light, shallow depth of field." Shot three: "Hands seasoning a pot, close-up, warm cinematic light." Shot four: "Friends laughing around a table, candid phone-cam style." Shot five: "Static shot of the restaurant sign with the logo." That is a script. Five lines. Each line is one prompt. Notice there is no story, no backstory, no paragraph — just five moments.

Section 5. Usable versus unusable prompts. The difference is specificity and restraint. A usable prompt has one subject, one action, a clear camera move, and a consistent style. An unusable prompt sounds like this: "A busy day at the restaurant with customers and food and music and the chef and a happy vibe, make it cinematic and viral." Two people, an abstract concept — happy vibe — and no camera direction at all. The AI cannot film a vibe. It can film a person laughing. So we film a person laughing.

Section 6. The tools will not save a bad script. Remember from lesson one: this space moves fast. The workflow you just learned works in Runway, Pika, Kling, and Veo — the names change, the shape does not. Check the four boxes from lesson one, and you are safe anywhere.

Wrap up. Here is your homework: write a 30-second script for a real product or service — yours or a client's. Five to six lines, one per shot. Each line must have a subject, an action, a camera move, and a style. In the next lesson, we take your script and generate real clips from it.
